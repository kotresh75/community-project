## 1. Survey Approach

This Literature Survey follows a **Hybrid 3-Layer + Gap Matrix** structure suited for a VTU `1BCP308` Community Project:
1. **Existing Public Portals (National + State/Local Utility Context):** PM Surya Ghar (MNRE), Unified GOBARdhan, BEE / MNRE *Akshay Urja*, plus state-level agency/DISCOM interfaces (e.g., KREDL and BESCOM in Karnataka).
2. **Commercial Solar & Energy Calculators:** Market-driven estimators.
3. **Peer-Reviewed Academic Literature:** Five verified studies on Indian adoption barriers, behavioral technology acceptance models, and digital community energy platforms.
4. **Comparative Gap Analysis & References:** Direct synthesis showing why our **Topic #40** portal is needed and how it differs from **Topic #14**.

## 2. Layer 1: Existing Government & Local Utility Portals

### 2.1 National Portals (India)
- **PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana — National Portal for Rooftop Solar (MNRE):**  
  Provides an end-to-end digital workflow for residential rooftop solar subsidies (Direct Benefit Transfer), vendor selection, financing links, and DISCOM net-metering tracking, alongside outreach such as "Model Solar Village" initiatives.  
  *Limitations for Community Adoption:* Geared primarily toward independent single-family rooftops, leaving multi-story apartments and group housing societies with shared-roof and common-meter hurdles. Standalone battery storage is excluded from standard subsidy workflows, DISCOM approvals add friction, and application-heavy English workflows intimidate elderly or less digitally fluent citizens.
- **Unified GOBARdhan Portal & BEE / MNRE *Akshay Urja*:**  
  Coordinate national waste-to-energy/biogas tracking (GOBARdhan), energy efficiency standards (Bureau of Energy Efficiency), and policy/case-study publications (*Akshay Urja*).  
  *Limitations for Community Adoption:* Designed largely for institutional reporting, compliance, or passive document reading rather than active household habit change. Dense techno-bureaucratic English creates a last-mile barrier, and post-setup grassroots maintenance or community drive coordination is limited.

### 2.2 State & Local Utility Context (Karnataka Example: KREDL & DISCOM / BESCOM Interfaces)
At the state level, renewable deployment involves coordination between nodal agencies such as **KREDL** (Karnataka Renewable Energy Development Limited), electricity regulatory frameworks (**KERC**), and local distribution utilities (**BESCOM / ESCOMs**) alongside central portals.  
*Observed Community-Level Limitations:*
- **Multi-Agency & Process Confusion:** Citizens frequently struggle to understand how central subsidies align with state utility rules, sanctioned-load limits, shade-free roof prerequisites, and documentation checks (such as property/Occupancy Certificate requirements and name-matching across utility records).
- **Low Motivation Beyond Pure Bill Savings:** When domestic consumers already face low or subsidized monthly electricity bills, purely ROI-focused pitches fail to motivate action—proving that communities need **non-financial environmental and sustainable-practice awareness** (efficiency habits, clean cooking, shared initiatives), not just bill calculators.
- **Apartment & Group Housing Gaps:** Shared residential complexes face internal consensus hurdles and unclear common-meter integration paths.

## 3. Layer 2: Commercial Solar & Energy Calculator Websites

Commercial vendor portals and online ROI tools provide quick bill-offset and rooftop sizing estimates.  
*Limitations for Topic #40:*
- **Commercial Bias:** Designed for vendor lead generation, which conflicts with `1BCP308`'s strict academic, politically neutral, and non-commercial conduct requirements.
- **Narrow Scope:** Focused almost exclusively on solar financial estimation (closer to **Topic #14**), ignoring broader sustainable practices such as biogas, clean cooking, reducing firewood/biomass dependency (**SDG 15**), and collective community drives.

## 4. Layer 3: Peer-Reviewed Academic Literature

### 4.1 Narrative Review of Key Studies
1. **Luthra, S., Garg, S., Garg, D., & Haleem, A. (2015)** studied barriers to renewable and sustainable energy technology adoption in the Indian context, categorizing and ranking **28 primary barriers** across awareness/information, technical, economic, financial, market, and policy dimensions. Their findings establish that lack of accessible, trustworthy information is a foundational bottleneck—directly justifying a plain-language, neutral awareness portal.
2. **Minuto, F. D., Lanzini, A., Giannuzzo, L., & Borchiellini, R. (2022)** reviewed digital platforms and geoportals supporting Renewable Energy Communities (RECs). They demonstrate that centralized digital tools streamline stakeholder coordination and project planning, while noting that most existing REC platforms target technical grid/asset management rather than everyday household education and practice adoption.
3. **Hill, M., & Duffy, A. (2022)** designed and evaluated an integrated "one-stop-shop" digital support platform for community energy. Their evaluation confirms that bringing educational resources and community action together in a single low-friction web portal improves community engagement and participation.
4. **Radtke, J., Yildiz, Ö., & Roth, L. (2022)** examined empirical evidence from community energy participation and found that active membership in community energy initiatives positively shifts participants' everyday sustainable attitudes and energy behaviors—supporting our combination of awareness guides with active community drives and personal pledges.
5. **Dubey, S. (2025)** synthesized the **Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)** and **Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)** among Indian households (Bihar) to evaluate solar adoption intent. Results highlight that **Perceived Usefulness**, **Perceived Ease of Use**, **Attitude**, and **Subjective Norms** strongly influence adoption behavior—reinforcing our decision to offer zero-friction public access, a **10-language regional toggle**, and visible community participation.

## 6. Baseline Synthesis & Conclusion

The literature and platform review reveals a clear **"Awareness-to-Action" gap**:
- **Government and local utility portals** (MNRE, GOBARdhan, BEE, KREDL/BESCOM) focus on formal subsidy applications, technical compliance, and institutional tracking, often leaving everyday households confused by multi-agency steps and technical English.
- **Commercial portals** focus narrowly on solar ROI calculations for sales leads, neglecting non-commercial community education, clean cooking/biogas, and land-resource conservation (**SDG 15**).
- **Academic findings** (Luthra et al., 2015; Hill & Duffy, 2022; Radtke et al., 2022; Dubey, 2025) consistently show that bridging awareness to adoption requires **high ease of use**, **local-language clarity**, **step-by-step practical readiness**, and **visible community participation**.

Therefore, our proposed **Topic #40** portal fills an unaddressed space: a **neutral, 10-language, non-commercial community hub** combining open renewable awareness, personal sustainable-practice checklists/pledges, and community initiative management (with a user-input practice impact estimator strictly as a secondary helper).
